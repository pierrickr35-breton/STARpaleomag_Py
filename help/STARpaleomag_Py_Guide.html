History and foreword

This program (STAR, for Système de Traitement des Aimantations Rémanentes — remanent magnetization processing system) has been built up over more than four decades.

In the early 1980s, researchers at the paleomagnetism laboratory of the Institut de Géologie de Rennes (N. Bonhommet, H. Perroud, P. Roperch, J-P. Cogné, A. Chauvin, among others) helped develop in-house programs for processing paleomagnetic data, which at the time was stored in binary formats. The growing power of computers eventually made it possible to leave those binary formats behind.

With the arrival of the Mac II in the late 1980s, I began building a Mac application (Starmac, for "star on Mac") with menus and graphics, first written in C. It wasn't until the 2000s that I returned to Fortran, because Absoft's Fortran compilers made it possible to build applications with graphical windows and menus (the MRWE, then AWE, environments).

When the Absoft company closed its doors in 2022, these applications were on track to disappear.

The rise of AI opened up a new way of working for me, and it was Anthropic's Claude that made it possible to rebuild and modernize these applications.

Concretely, the rebuild means the Fortran/AWE codebase (Absoft-only, tied to a compiler and a windowing toolkit that no longer exist) has become a plain Python 3 application: Tkinter for the menus, dialogs and windows (in place of AWE), Matplotlib for every plot (Zijderveld, stereonet, Arai diagram, IRM/susceptibility curves), and a direct dependency on PmagPy — the community paleomagnetism library — wherever a MagIC-standard statistic is involved (Hext F-test for anisotropy, MAD/DANG/FRAC/fvds for paleointensity), so those numbers match what any other MagIC-aware tool would compute. The result runs unmodified on macOS, Windows and Linux, with no Absoft runtime or licence to install. Wherever the original Fortran encoded a real scientific choice — an algorithm, a convention — the port follows it deliberately rather than silently "modernizing" the numbers.

None of this is something a user of the software ever needs to know. Being written in Python matters for whoever wants to read, extend or fix the code — it has no bearing on how the program is used day to day: you launch it, get the same menus, dialogs and plots described in this guide, and work through them exactly as before, with no Python (or Fortran) knowledge required.

Open source, and an invitation. The source code is available on GitHub, alongside its sibling application for anisotropy of magnetic susceptibility, AMS_Py. I strongly encourage researchers and students who wish to use and modify these programs to do so through Claude — above all, it is what opened up a whole new world of programming for me.

— P. Roperch

A way of working

This software covers most of what Demag_GUI and Thellier_GUI offer, and for a lot of everyday processing it's arguably a simpler way to work with the data than going through Jupyter notebooks. paleomagnetism.org can also visualize some of the same data, but not paleointensity data.

Demag_GUI and paleomagnetism.org both take a fundamentally specimen-by-specimen approach: one specimen loaded, interpreted, and moved past before the next. STARpaleomag_Py can work that way too — select, visualize and process one specimen at a time is the default rhythm of best lines…, the Arai diagram, and so on. But Select site… combined with Select data…'s step-range filter (see Selection) lets you build a selection spanning an entire site, narrowed to one particular temperature or AF interval — and several plots in the Graphics menu then draw that whole group together on one plot rather than one specimen at a time: Stereo data, XYgraph and Susceptibility each take the full selection and plot one curve or point set per specimen. The Zijderveld plot is the exception — it's deliberately per-specimen, one diagram at a time, since it's where you actually pick step ranges and fit a line or plane.

Why look at a site all at once. That group view works in every orientation — specimen coordinates as well as in-situ and tilt-corrected (see Orientation (SC/IS/TC)) — and specimen coordinates specifically are where equipment-level problems tend to become visible: a magnetization systematically picked up during AF demagnetization (see GRM), or a strong residual field left in a thermal furnace, both show up as a shared, non-random pattern across specimens in the instrument's own frame — a pattern easy to miss specimen by specimen, but obvious once several specimens are plotted together in the frame where the equipment itself operates.

Selecting a whole site within one temperature or field interval also gives a fast read on how a group of specimens actually behaves during demagnetization — not the fine detail of any one specimen's fit, but the shared shape of the group's response, which is its own kind of useful signal in paleomagnetism, not just a shortcut for going through specimens faster one by one.

Recording susceptibility at every thermal step. It's worth measuring magnetic susceptibility when measuring the NRM, and including after each thermal-demagnetization step, not only at the start — .prmag has always carried a susceptibility column (s) per measurement, and Susceptibility in the Graphics menu plots it against step for the whole selection at once. Few laboratories actually measure it this systematically, but it's genuinely worth the extra reading. Magnetic susceptibility is a good proxy for the amount and nature of the magnetic minerals in the sample. A susceptibility jump partway through a heating sequence is often the clearest sign that a mineralogical transformation happened during that heating step, before the direction or intensity data alone would show anything unusual. This matters particularly in sedimentary rocks carrying sulfides (which readily alter to magnetite on heating) and in volcanic rocks carrying maghemite (which inverts to hematite at a characteristic temperature, with a strong decrease in magnetic susceptibility) — cases where the susceptibility curve can flag exactly which chemical changes happened in the sample.

— P. Roperch

Data formats: .prmag, MagIC, and Utrecht/PMAG2

STARpaleomag_Py works internally on a single, uniform format — .prmag — no matter where the data originally came from. MagIC and Utrecht/PMAG2 are external formats you import from: STARpaleomag_Py converts them to .prmag once, on load, and never touches their original files again. .prmag is not "the MagIC format" renamed — it is STARpaleomag_Py's own working format, close enough to MagIC's conventions to make round-tripping straightforward, but with its own header, units, and one-block-per-specimen layout (full column reference: .prmag file format).

FormatWhat it isStructureRelationship to STARpaleomag_Py
.prmag STARpaleomag_Py's own internal working format Plain text, one block per specimen (4 header lines + a tab-separated measurement table), angles in degrees, fields in mT/µT, temperatures in °C Native. Everything in the app — fitting, plots, paleointensity, anisotropy — reads and writes this format directly. Every other format is converted to this one before STARpaleomag_Py can use it.
MagIC The community's shared archival/publication database (earthref.org/MagIC) — the standard for depositing and re-using published paleomagnetic data Several separate tables (sites.txt, samples.txt, specimens.txt, measurements.txt, locations.txt), one row per record, SI units (Tesla, Kelvin) Import and export. Import MagIC contribution file… converts a downloaded contribution to .prmag (see the field-mapping table under .ren file format); export Rennes to Magic… goes the other way, to deposit results.
Utrecht / PMAG2 The file format of paleomagnetism.org's PMAG2 interpretation tool (Utrecht University) — despite the .col extension, it is JSON One file per batch (often several sites at once), specimens already resolved to x/y/z in the specimen frame, optionally carrying PMAG2's own line/plane interpretations Import only. Converts to .prmag, plus a companion .pmagres when the file carries interpretations — see the notes below.
Why not just use MagIC everywhere? MagIC's table-per-record layout is built for archiving and exchange, not for the interactive, one-specimen-at-a-time work STARpaleomag_Py is built around — Zijderveld fitting, Arai diagrams, removing a bad step and re-numbering the rest. .prmag keeps everything about one specimen together in one readable block, in the units actually read off the lab instruments (mT/µT/°C, not Tesla/Kelvin) — the MagIC/Utrecht conversion happens once, at the file boundary, not throughout the app.

Utrecht/PMAG2 conversion notes — three points worth knowing if you compare a re-imported specimen against PMAG2's own display:

  • No site concept. PMAG2 dropped the notion of "site" entirely (one sample = one site, by design — not a STARpaleomag_Py choice), and a specimen name like BN4.1A carries no reliable site separator. STARpaleomag_Py uses the .col file's own name as the site (a file can bundle several real sites, e.g. SR1-3-4-10-11.col — split manually afterwards in STARpaleomag_Py if that matters for your dataset).
  • Core orientation. PMAG2's coreAzimuth/coreDip use a different convention from STARpaleomag_Py's own caz/cin: caz = coreAzimuth + 90 (same rule as the MagIC azimuth convention below), cin = 90 − coreDip. Verified against 156 real interpretations from 4 Utrecht files: reprojecting the specimen-frame vector with this rule reproduces PMAG2's own geographic-frame vector to better than 0.9999 (cosine) in 155 of 156 cases — the one exception is an exact antipode, a known PCA eigenvector sign ambiguity, not a formula error.
  • Magnetization units. PMAG2 stores x/y/z already divided by volume (µA/m, a magnetization) rather than the raw moment in Am² that .prmag expects — reconverted on import (moment = µA/m × volume(cm³) × 10⁻¹²).

MagIC conversion notes — the conventions applied when importing (see the full column table under .prmag file format for every field): azimuth in .prmag is the azimuth of the specimen's X axis plus 90° relative to MagIC's own azimuth; dip is the negative of the X axis's dip from horizontal; and MagIC's bed_dip_direction becomes STARpaleomag_Py's bed_dip_strike + 90. The per-measurement quality column (g/b) is read straight from MagIC's own quality field in measurements.txt — MagIC already carries this exact g/b convention per measurement, so a specimen already flagged b on some steps in the source contribution arrives into .prmag already flagged, ready for Remove bad-quality (b) steps… without having to re-identify the problem steps by hand; only when MagIC's own field is absent does it fall back to n.d. Utrecht/PMAG2 carries no such flag at all, so that import path has nothing to read and marks every step g unconditionally — mark individual steps b afterwards in STARpaleomag_Py as you identify problems (see .pmagint).

Typical session flow

Most interpretations follow the same sequence, whatever the dataset:

  1. LoadPmagFile → Open pmag file… (or a MagIC import)
  2. SelectPmag data → Select data… / Select site…
  3. VisualizeGraphics → Zijderveld / Stereo data
  4. InterpretCalcul → best lines… / best planes…
  5. ExportPmagFile → export… or Graphics → Export SVG…
Current orientation (SC / IS / TC). All displayed directions — in plots as well as in List results — are recomputed live according to the orientation selected in Pmag data: Sample (raw specimen frame), In situ (drilling correction) or Tilt cor. (plus bedding-tilt correction). Values are always stored in the specimen frame; changing the orientation changes nothing in memory, only the display.
Keyboard shortcuts. Shown in parentheses after some labels, e.g. Cmd+E. Always Cmd or Cmd+Ctrl — never Ctrl alone, to avoid conflicting with the input field's own editing shortcuts.

PmagFile files

Load data, import from MagIC, export to various formats.

Import

Open Pmag file… Cmd+O
Loads a .prmag file (or a legacy .ren, read as-is — see .prmag file format). Resets the selection and locates the associated .pmagres file (results) in the same folder.
Import Starmac legacy files…
Upgrades an old Rennes-format data file — 1, 2 or 3 header lines per specimen, even mixed within the same file — straight to the current 3-line header format, then on to .prmag, in a single step (previously two separate commands). An external "complement" table can fill in Site/Formation/Age/Lithology alongside, but it's optional: without one, or for a specimen it doesn't cover, the conversion still proceeds, with Not Specified in the geology fields rather than blocking the import — exactly what Complete sample information… is for afterwards. Each measurement's one- or two-character legacy instrument code is spelled out into a readable instrument field: S→Schonstedt, M…→Molspin spinner, C1→2G magnetometer one position, C4→2G magnetometer 4 positions, J/J?→Agico Spinner, J5/J6→Agico Spinner Jr5/Jr6 (distinct from JA/J2, which name the measurement protocol — automatic vs. 2-position — not the instrument model).
Import MagIC contribution to .prmag format…
Converts a combined MagIC contribution file (as downloaded from earthref.org — all tables in a single .txt) into a .prmag file. Site/Formation/Age/Lithology fields (and, when present, stratigraphic_height) are decoded automatically and remain available on each specimen. Several companion files are generated in the same pass, whenever the contribution actually carries the corresponding data: the .pmagres file (already-interpreted directions from specimens.txt/sites.txt, re-fit through STARpaleomag_Py's own native fit rather than copied as-is, plus site means archived as given), a redo_pint_contribution_<num>.txt file — one specimen tmin tmax cooling aniso_corr line per quality-g paleointensity determination found in specimens.txt (method_codes carrying LP-PI-TRM, or the generic LP-PI tag some contributions use instead for the very same thermal Thellier/IZZI protocol), directly usable with View batch of Paleoint Results… to replay every paleointensity interval the contribution already reports — and, when specimens.txt already carries a pre-computed anisotropy tensor (aniso_s, from an ATRM/AARM/AMS experiment run by any lab or software, not just this one), a matching .pmagani row (A0 for TRM, AA for ARM, N0 for susceptibility).
Import Utrecht/PMAG2 .col to .prmag format…
Converts one or more .col files from paleomagnetism.org's PMAG2 tool (Utrecht University — JSON despite the extension) into a single combined .prmag, plus a companion .pmagres for any interpretations PMAG2 already computed. See Data formats above for the conventions this conversion relies on (site naming, core-orientation formula, magnetization units) and how well they've been verified against real data.
Import IPGP measurements/results to .prmag format… new
Converts raw specimen-orientation measurement files from IPGP (Johan Guyodo's format, and the closely related CryoMag/PaleoMac .pmd export) and/or a separate "Results" CSV of already-interpreted directions (Dragomir Dragomirov's format) into .prmag/.pmagres. Core azimuth/dip conventions are verified numerically against the file's own Dg/Ig/Ds/Is columns rather than assumed; AF vs. thermal demagnetization is read straight from a step-label prefix (T…/A…) when present, falling back to a majority-of-steps heuristic otherwise.
Import Montpellier .montpellier to .prmag format… new
Converts the flat Montpellier table format (one row per measurement: Sample Step Intensity Dec Inc Azimuth Plunge Strike Dip) to .prmag. Column semantics were cross-checked against an independently-verified Utrecht/PMAG2 import of the same specimen collection rather than guessed. This format carries no per-specimen volume, so a default (10.8 cm³, the same standard core-plug value used elsewhere in this app) is assumed and can be overridden at import time — it only affects the displayed absolute intensity, never the fitted directions.
Orientation: field notes orientation to prmag file… new
Turns a raw field-orientation notebook — one line per core, with a magnetic-compass azimuth and, whenever a sun sight was also taken, a sun-compass azimuth and the clock time it was read at — into a ready .prmag (no measurements yet, an instrument hasn't touched these cores), a single .ged file combining every site for AGICO instruments, and a per-core diagnostic report. An empty companion .pmagani (header only) is created alongside the .prmag if one doesn't already exist — ready to archive AMS data into (AMS_Py's Archive ASC into .pmagani…) as soon as it's measured, without waiting for that first archive to bring the file into existence.
Why sight the same core twice. On the outcrop, the same reference mark on a core is sighted with two independent methods: a magnetic compass, and — when conditions allow — a sun-compass reading against a known clock time. The sun-compass azimuth is purely astronomical: it depends only on the site's latitude/longitude and the date/time, never on the Earth's magnetic field, so comparing it to the magnetic-compass reading gives the actual local magnetic declination at that spot on that day. That's valuable precisely where a global model can't help: a metal tool near the compass, or a real magnetic anomaly in the outcrop itself, would bias the magnetic-only reading without ever showing up as a discrepancy against a smooth model — only a second, independent sighting catches it. This locally-measured declination is a diagnostic, never applied automatically to correct a magnetic-only core.
IGRF: correction when there's no sun sight, cross-check when there is. For every core, the import also computes the declination predicted by the IGRF (International Geomagnetic Reference Field, the standard global geomagnetic model) for that site's coordinates and sampling date. The two data-availability cases are handled differently: for a core with no sun sight at all, there's nothing to compare against, so the IGRF declination is applied directly to the magnetic-compass azimuth to get the true geographic orientation — the best available estimate, not a diagnostic. For a core that does have a sun sight, the geographic azimuth is instead computed purely astronomically from the sun sighting itself, and IGRF is only held up against that independent result for comparison — it is never allowed to override a real sun-sight measurement. Close agreement in that second case confirms the compass reading, the sun sight, and the recorded date/time are all consistent; a sizeable gap usually flags a data-entry mistake rather than a real anomaly — most often a wrong UT time-zone offset on the sun sight, which the import checks automatically by testing whether a different offset would have matched IGRF far better, and reports as a warning when it finds one. The diagnostic report lists every sun-sighted core by increasing severity (err>3/5/10°) so the worst offenders are easy to spot. This build already uses IGRF-14 (2025) — via PmagPy, which loads its coefficients from a plain text file refreshed every five years, rather than hard-coded values that would need a recompile to update.
Create prmag from AGICO .ged file… new
An alternative starting point when only a .ged is available — received from a colleague, or produced by the AGICO instrument's own software — rather than the raw field-orientation notebook above. Reads each specimen's id, core azimuth/dip and bedding strike/dip straight from the .ged's fixed-column layout and writes a .prmag with no measurements yet; site, date, geology, volume/mass are left as n.d/defaults (fill them in afterwards with Complete sample information…). Same empty companion .pmagani creation as the field-notes import above. The natural next step is Archive new laboratory measurements below, which attaches the instrument's real measurements to these specimens — it requires them to already exist in the target .prmag, which is exactly what this command prepares.
Verify the orientation convention. AGICO instruments record azimuth/dip against a configurable convention (REMA6W manual §12.2's P1–P4 parameters — arrow direction, which angle counts as inclination, which compass direction counts as azimuth, dip-direction vs. strike/dip for bedding), declared in the .ged's own header. This command reads that declaration and transforms accordingly, warning explicitly whenever it finds a value it can't fully verify or doesn't recognize — but a plain "no warning" only means the file's own declared convention looked internally consistent, not that the file actually used this lab's usual convention correctly in the first place. For a .ged that didn't come from this app — a colleague's file, or one exported straight from the AGICO instrument — check the header's declared parameters against the manual by hand before trusting the result, especially for an older file: this is exactly the kind of mismatch that has caused real errors before (a past Utrecht-to-MagIC export).
Complete sample information…
Patches geology, coordinates and stratigraphic position into a .prmag already loaded, from an external tab-separated table — filled in progressively as more becomes known, in as many passes as you like, rather than requiring everything up front at import time or re-doing the original import. Three modes, keyed by site, specimen or sample (see below). Only patches the raw text of the four header lines directly: whatever isn't in the table (measurements, method_codes, formatting of untouched values) stays byte-for-byte as it was. A .bak backup of the whole file is written once, the first time this command changes anything — never overwritten afterwards, so it always holds the state from before the very first completion pass. Reports, in a companion _completion_report.txt file (the full list, not truncated), both the specimens that had no matching row in the table and — separately — any specimen that still has at least one field left at n.d after this pass, whether from a missing match or a table that only supplied some of its columns.
Three independent modes, three table shapes. Which one to use depends on how much you already trust the .prmag's own sample:/site: fields, and whether you're filling shared metadata or a per-sample position.
  • Site mode — assumes sample/site are already correct in the .prmag (typically true after a MagIC import, or a legacy conversion that derived the site cleanly from the specimen name). One row per site, applied to every specimen of that site at once:
    #site lat lon formation age geologic_classes geologic_types lithologies location obs
  • Specimen mode — assumes nothing reliable about sample/site; the table supplies (and overwrites) those too, and also accepts a stratigraphic_height column, one row per specimen:
    #specimen sample site lat lon stratigraphic_height formation age geologic_classes geologic_types lithologies location obs
  • Sample mode new — a narrow special case for exactly one field, stratigraphic_height: several specimens (A/B/C…) are routinely cut from the very same physical sample, and necessarily share the very same position in the section, so repeating that value specimen by specimen in a table is pure duplication. This mode takes one row per sample instead, and applies it to every specimen sharing that sample:
    #sample stratigraphic_height

In all three modes, every column may be left empty for any given row — only cells that actually carry a value overwrite anything, so a table can supply just formation and age today and lithologies next month without disturbing what was already filled in. The leading # on the header line is optional, and column names tolerate spaces in place of underscores ("geologic classes" reads the same as geologic_classes). Site mode never accepts stratigraphic_height — a site combines multiple samples at different positions, so a single site-wide value would be wrong by construction.

Building the table: several ways to get there. Nothing requires typing a table from a blank page.
  • Start from the specimen list already on screen. Loading any .prmag/.ren prints one line per specimen straight into the console (ID: … | Measurements: … | Inc: … | Az: …) — select and copy that block into a text editor or spreadsheet and it's already one row per specimen, in the file's own order. Strip the columns you don't need, add site and whatever geology columns you're ready to fill, and you have the start of a specimen-mode table without retyping a single specimen name by hand.
  • Two passes instead of one. Use specimen mode first for the one thing only a per-specimen table can do — assigning (or correcting) the site each specimen belongs to. Once every specimen has the right site, switch to site mode for a second, much shorter table: one row per site rather than repeating the same formation/age/lithologies string on every specimen that shares it.
  • Let a spreadsheet do the repetitive part. With the specimen list in a spreadsheet, sort or filter by whatever the specimen naming convention already encodes (a site prefix, a sampling-day block…) and fill the site column down a whole contiguous group at once, instead of assigning it specimen by specimen.
The single most valuable step before a MagIC export. export to Magic database (below) can only export what the .prmag already knows — a site with no formation/age/lithologies, or a specimen with no stratigraphic_height, exports exactly that gap into MagIC. Since none of this metadata has to be known at import time (see "Nothing has to be filled in"), Complete sample information… is what actually closes those gaps afterwards, from whatever table you can put together — field notebooks, a spreadsheet from a colleague, literature values for a formation's age — without re-importing anything or hand-editing the .prmag text. Run it as many times as new information arrives, right up until the export.
Archive new laboratory measurements
Attaches measurements acquired after a .prmag was already created — the same specimens went back on the magnetometer, or the JR6 spinner, for another round — without rebuilding the file from scratch. Accepts either a new legacy Rennes-format file or a raw JR6 file (one or several at once, merged before archiving); a measurement whose (step, cod1, cod2) already exists for that specimen is skipped rather than duplicated, so re-running the import on a file that partly overlaps earlier data is safe. A specimen in the new data with no match in the target .prmag is logged and left out — this command only ever adds measurements to specimens that already exist, never creates new ones. Patches the file in place (new lines only ever appended at the end of the right specimen's block; a .bak backup is written once, the first time).
Check files for duplicate entries… new
Scans the loaded .prmag and its companion .pmagres/.pmagani/.pmagint files for duplicate rows (same specimen/step for measurements, same specimen for an archived result or anisotropy tensor) — a common symptom of re-running an import or archive step twice. Reports what it finds; nothing is deleted automatically.

Export

export to Magic database
Generates the MagIC tables (sites, samples, specimens, measurements, locations) from the current selection and saved results. Each sites row gets one line per archived mean (in situ and tilt-corrected, if both were saved), and its specimens column lists the real specimen names contributing to that mean — see Select results… for how that list is resolved, and why site mode is the more exact choice on a legacy file. Any .pmagani specimen or site-mean tensor marked export=N (see .pmagani columns) is skipped here — the curated selection made in AMS_Py's own Mark selection for MagIC export… is respected without needing an intermediate file.
export pmag content as a text file
Detailed text file: a full parameter block per specimen (site, orientation, age, geology…) followed by the measurement table in all three frames (specimen / in-situ / after tilt correction). Includes each specimen's magnetostratigraphic height whenever it's known — from stratigraphic_height in the .prmag when any selected specimen has it set, otherwise (older files) by asking "with magnetostratigraphic data" and offering a one-off sample/height file, same as before.
export results to Stereo_Py…
Writes two files, in two different formats, both directly usable by StereoUtils_Py rather than a generic text dump: a "Project" file — the saved fits, grouped into a "layer" per site (see StereoUtils_Py's own guide, Project menu, for why that grouping is worth having rather than one flat list) — loadable as-is via StereoUtils_Py's own Project → Load Project…. A site's layer lets "Fisher Project" recompute that site's mean directly from these specimen directions, and — once plotted and exported to SVG — becomes its own named, independently toggleable layer in Illustrator/Inkscape, useful for composing a final stereonet figure out of many overlaid sites. A companion _poles file carries the same site means as VGPs (with their dp/dm confidence ellipse) in the "Data input" format expected by Plot VGPs on Map — a pole isn't a local direction, so it needs its own file and format, not the Project one.

Pmag data selection

Choose which specimens/measurements are active for the Results, Paleointensity, Calcul and Graphics menus, and in which orientation they are displayed.

Selection

Select data… Cmd+E
Filters by specimen number (*/? wildcards), demagnetization code, and step range. Adds to the existing selection rather than replacing it — use Init list to start over. The demagnetization code is asked before the step range so the Step min/max prompts can label and accept the right unit: mT for an AF code (A/F), degC for a thermal code (D/S/T/K) — type the same value you'd see in List data, no conversion needed on your end.
Select site… new
Selects all specimens from a given MagIC site (decoded from import metadata). Lists available sites, also adds to the existing selection. Same demag-code-first, unit-aware Step min/max prompts as Select data….
Select header… Cmd+Ctrl+E
Sets a prefix automatically applied to every specimen number subsequently entered in Select data….
Delete some data… Cmd+D
Removes specific measurements from the current selection (by step/range), without touching the loaded file. Same demag-code-first, unit-aware Step min/max prompts as Select data….
Init list Cmd+I
Clears the current selection.

Lists & info

List data Cmd+L
Text table of the selection's measurements: specimen, step (with its unit — °C thermal, mT AF — and treatment code), total moment, a single intensity column normalized by volume (A/m) or mass (Am2/kg) — whichever the selection actually uses, not both with a placeholder for the one that doesn't apply — declination/inclination, quality, instrument, and susceptibility (K, unit following the same volume/mass choice).
List in XYZ
Same measurements, presented in raw X/Y/Z Cartesian coordinates (normalized by volume or mass — whichever the selection uses — with the resulting unit stated once, next to Z, rather than repeated per column).
List and depth…
Lists measurements against each specimen's position in the section — the angle to an expected site direction included, for stratigraphic-section or drill-core studies. Uses the .prmag stratigraphic_height: field directly whenever at least one selected specimen has it set; only asks for an external specimen/depth file as a fallback for older files that don't have it yet.
Info samples Cmd+J
Summary sheet per specimen: steps, lithology, orientation, volume/mass, site coordinates — plus Decli_IGRF and Decli_local. Decli_IGRF is the geomagnetic model's declination at that specimen's site and sampling date; Decli_local is the declination actually measured on-site from the magnetic- and sun-compass azimuth pair recorded for that core (see Orientation: field notes…) — n.d whenever no sun sight was taken. The two columns sitting side by side make a specimen where they disagree by more than a few degrees easy to spot at a glance.
Worth a look on every dataset. Viscous remanent magnetization (VRM)-oriented list — variation between consecutive measurements, plus elapsed hours since the specimen's first measurement. Grouped here with Test viscosity rather than the Pmag data listing commands, since both are about the same VRM concern.
Émilie Thellier's viscosity test, and why it's worth reviving. This is the test Émilie Thellier defined in the 1950s: leave the specimens for a set period — with X aligned north — then, after two weeks or a month, rotate them 180° about the Y axis and measure again. The N+/N- code pair records exactly that pair of measurements. Averaging the two isolates the residual NRM (any VRM acquired during the wait cancels between the two opposite orientations); their difference isolates the VRM itself, the magnetization actually picked up over that month or two weeks. The test was routine practice at Rennes in the early 1980s, then fell out of use in recent decades as it became rare across the paleomagnetism community generally — very likely a mistake (see Westerweel et al., 2025, GSL Special Publication).

Lists the difference vectors between consecutive measurements (GRM/noise diagnostic).

Anisotropy new
Detects the 6 TRM/ARM positions automatically, writes all 15 tensor variants (A0 main, plus 14 jackknife estimates) to the specimen's .pmagani file, and runs a parallel PmagPy Hext F-test — if the result is not statistically significant, you're asked whether to save it anyway.
The 6-position procedure, and why not fewer. Determining the anisotropy of a remanent magnetization — TRM or ARM — takes six separate magnetizing/measuring cycles per specimen, in the six orientations X+, X−, Y+, Y−, Z+, Z−: a genuinely time-consuming step. In principle three would be enough — with no residual NRM at all, one vector measurement along each of the three axes (X+, Y+, Z+ only) already fixes the tensor's action on those axes. The reason to measure all six anyway is that the official A0 tensor is built pair by pair, from the half-difference (position+ − position−)/2 of each axis — a shortcut that cancels out anything shared by both members of a pair without having to know or assume its size, above all a residual NRM the demagnetization sequence left behind. That's also why this procedure can be run partway through a paleointensity experiment, once a substantial fraction of the NRM has already been replaced by a partial TRM, and not only on a specimen fully demagnetized beforehand — the pairing cancels the residual either way. STARpaleomag_Py's own detection and .pmagani writer are both built around the full 6-position design end to end, so a run with fewer than six real, distinct measurements isn't something the software can process, whatever the theoretical minimum. The order of the six measurements matters little in principle — unless the specimen risks altering partway through the sequence, which is exactly the situation in a paleointensity run where a TRM is being progressively acquired. For that case, an optional seventh, control measurement (a repeat of the Z position, coded ZB, at the same step as the X measurements) lets the dialog flag apparent evolution above 5% and, on request, substitute that control measurement for the original Z+ before the tensor is computed.
Spotting and swapping out a bad measurement. Watch that the residual NRM comes out consistent whether it's estimated from the X, Y or Z pair on its own — unless the specimen carries a full TRM, in which case there's no shared residual NRM left to compare in the first place. Beyond that, each pair's TRM should be antiparallel: the X+/Y+/Z+ readings should point opposite to their X-/Y-/Z- partners. A strong angular deviation within a pair is often a genuine red flag — it usually points to the specimen having been poorly positioned in the furnace, or to a magnetometer calibration problem, rather than noise to shrug off. If you don't spot anything wrong — which should be the normal case — pressing Return answers every question below with its default, so a clean specimen moves through the whole routine in a few keystrokes.
What the dialog actually prints, and how a swap is computed. Three listings are printed before the tensor itself, each narrowing down where a problem might sit. First, the residual NRM estimated from each pair on its own (X+X-, Y+Y-, Z+Z-) — before they're averaged into the single "Mean NRM" used everywhere else — since a residual NRM that's supposed to be common to all six positions but instead sits well apart on just one pair points at that specific pair, not a general contamination affecting the whole specimen. Then the six dec/inc/intensity TRM values, and the deviation percentage — the classic tell there is one position whose intensity or direction sits oddly apart from its opposite partner, pushing the deviation up. After that listing, the dialog asks which of the six numbered lines to remove (1-6, matching the order already printed just above, or N to move on). Picking one does not ask for a replacement measurement to substitute — the flagged position is reconstructed instead from the two pieces of the data least likely to share its problem: its own pair partner (the antiparallel measurement of the same orientation) and the average residual NRM of the other two pairs, on the assumption that a pair should, in the absence of a problem, agree with the other two on that shared NRM level. Concretely, for a flagged Y+: Y+ = −Y− + 2 × average(X-pair mean, Z-pair mean). The whole tensor is then recomputed from that corrected set of six (residual NRM, any X/Y inversion check, the ZB evolution check — everything depends on the six positions, so all of it reruns, not just the one reconstructed position). The dialog allows repeating this for more than one position, but that's not recommended — reconstructing two positions at once means the tensor now rests on only four real measurements, and the internal cross-checks (the deviation percentage, the X/Y inversion check) lose most of their power to catch a second real problem. If more than one position looks suspect, that's a sign to question the specimen's positioning or the whole measurement run rather than to reconstruct your way past it.
"Not significant" often means imprecise, not weak. PmagPy's verdict is significant or not significant. It's tempting to read "not significant" as "this specimen's anisotropy is genuinely weak and unimportant" — but in practice it's often instead a sign that the six measurements themselves weren't precise enough. Orienting a cylindrical specimen accurately for the X, -X, Y, -Y positions is a real practical difficulty; presenting the determination as 14 extra jackknife tensors, rather than only the one A0 result, is precisely what lets you see that scatter for what it is. Where it's possible — pottery fragments, for example — quartz, cubic sample holders give a much more precise orientation in the paleointensity furnace than the cylindrical case, and are standard practice in Rennes and Madrid for exactly this reason. Alongside PmagPy's own significant/not significant, .pmagani's quality column and its "satisfactory"/"not satisfactory" wording are our own addition on top (see how the fourteen variants are built).
Anisotropy PmagPy… new
A second, independent computation of the same 6-position anisotropy data, calling PmagPy's own least-squares routines (Hext 1963) directly rather than STARpaleomag_Py's native pairwise calculation — display only, it never writes to .pmagani. Shown side by side with the native A0 tensor to confirm the two genuinely compute the same thing (verified on synthetic data); PmagPy's addition on top is the formal Hext F-test for statistical significance, which the native jackknife calculation doesn't provide.
Holder_ARM…
Records the 6 ARM measurements of an empty sample holder, used as a correction baseline for anisotropy.
Inverse_ANI_correction…
Applies the inverse of a chosen anisotropy tensor — TRM (A0), ARM (F0), susceptibility (N0), or an ARM tensor imported from a MagIC contribution (AA, see .pmagani) — from the specimen's .pmagani file, to all its measurements, and lists the corrected directions.
Detect GRM… new
Screens the selection for likely GRM (gyroremanent magnetization) contamination during AF demagnetization: fits a line through the usable AF steps, checks whether each step's residual from that line sits perpendicular to the last coil axis used at that step, and whether that perpendicular component grows with the applied AF field — the geometric and temporal signature of a real GRM, as opposed to noise or a second component. A diagnostic only, meant to flag specimens for a visual (Zijderveld) check — it changes nothing in the data.
Suppress GRM
Reduces a specimen's GRM measurement triplets — three consecutive points along X, Y, Z after the same AF step — to a single corrected point, either axis-by-axis substitution or a simple average of the three, matching the two Fortran variants this was ported from.
GRM is under-recognized, and it isn't a greigite thing. Spurious magnetization acquired during AF demagnetization was already described in the early 1980s (Dankers & Zijderveld, 1981; Roperch & Taylor, 1986), yet GRM is rarely discussed in paleomagnetism papers, and many users don't expect to encounter it at all — in sedimentary rock as much as in volcanic rock, for instance. In most cases the mineral actually recording it is ordinary magnetite, not greigite. Since the 2G cryogenic magnetometer and its three-axis AF demagnetizer, in line with the magnetometer, were installed in Rennes, demagnetization sequences have been deliberately programmed to never finish along the same coil axis twice in a row — and, especially toward the higher AF steps, a measurement is taken after each individual axis rather than only at the end of the full 3-axis step. A GRM's signature (perpendicular to the last axis used, growing with field) only becomes visible this way; a lab that always demagnetizes in the same fixed axis order can carry a GRM through an entire specimen without ever recognizing it — and often ends up mistaking this spurious magnetization for a genuine high-coercivity secondary component. These measurement sequences are now identified within MagIC's own method-code vocabulary, and STARpaleomag_Py naturally reads and exports them correctly, since the underlying cod1/cod2 convention they map from is the same one used throughout the app:

.prmag file format reference

STARpaleomag_Py's current internal text format — one plain-text file, one block per specimen — and how MagIC contribution fields map onto it on import.

.ren is legacy. .ren was the internal format of the previous AWE application (the Absoft/Qt-based predecessor to this port) — a single free-text key-value line per header field (Id:, az:, Site:…). It still opens in STARpaleomag_Py for backward compatibility, but every import path now writes .prmag, and .prmag is what every other file in this reference (.pmagres, .pmagani, .pmagint) is a companion to. Treat .ren as read-only history, not a format to target for anything new.
Nothing has to be filled in. Every field below can be written as n.d ("not determined") instead of a real value, and read back that way without breaking anything downstream — a specimen with no known volume, no sampling date, or no geologic age is just as valid a .prmag record as a fully-documented one. This is deliberate: it's what lets Import MagIC contribution file… and Import Utrecht .col… both write the same format even though the two sources rarely agree on which fields they actually populate, and it leaves the door open for a future importer that knows even less about its source data. A concrete example already in the code: if a MagIC contribution has neither volume nor weight for a specimen, the import doesn't fail or silently guess — it writes a standard 10.8 cm³ placeholder and records "no volume" in the specimen's own comment field, so the gap stays visible instead of being absorbed.

.pmagres file format reference

Companion of .prmag (same base name, same folder). Every line-fit or plane-fit you archive from the Zijderveld tools, and every Fisher site mean you build from them, is appended here as one plain-text row — nothing is ever overwritten in place, and nothing is ever silently rewritten from scratch.

Two sections, one file. .pmagres holds two tab-free, space-aligned tables under two #-prefixed headers: #specimen results in sample coordinates first, then #site mean results. A new specimen result is inserted at the end of the first section (just before the mean-results header, if one already exists) — never appended after the site means the way the original Fortran did. A new site mean always goes at the very end. Blank lines and any !-prefixed line you've typed in by hand (e.g. ! primary magnetization) are left exactly where they are on every rewrite.

.pmagani file format reference

Companion of .prmag (same base name, same folder). One row per anisotropy tensor variant, written by Anisotropy tensor… and read back by Inverse ANI correction… and the paleointensity dialogs.

15 rows per computation, not one. Every time Anisotropy tensor… succeeds, it appends fifteen rows for the same specimen/step in one go: A0 — the official, symmetrized tensor everything else uses — plus 14 jackknife estimates (A+, A-, A1/B1…A6/B6), built by recombining the same six measurements in different ways, kept for anyone who wants to assess how sensitive the tensor is to a single orientation. Only A0 is ever read back automatically by the correction tools.
How the fourteen variants are built. All fifteen rows come from the same six position vectors — nothing is re-measured or dropped. They fall into two families:
  • A0 / A+ / A- — for each axis, take the half-difference of its pair, (plus − minus)/2, which cancels out anything shared by both members of a pair (a residual NRM, an instrumental offset). The three half-difference vectors form the rows of a 3×3 matrix that isn't necessarily symmetric — A0 is that matrix forced symmetric, by averaging each pair of off-diagonal terms (k12, k23, k13); A+ and A- are the same numbers before averaging, one keeping the upper triangle, the other the lower — comparing them shows how far apart the two triangles were, i.e. how much forcing symmetry actually changed.
  • A1/B1 through A6/B6 — six more variants in three pairs, one pair per axis. For, say, the X axis: average X+ and X− together first (an estimate of whatever's common to that pair — background or residual NRM, the same signal the half-difference in A0 cancels), then subtract that average from the three plus-position vectors (X+, Y+, Z+) to get A1, or from the three minus-position vectors, sign-flipped, to get A2; B1/B2 are the same two numbers again with the opposite off-diagonal triangle kept, exactly as A+/A- relate to A0. A3/B3 and A4/B4 repeat this using the Y pair's average instead of X's; A5/B5 and A6/B6 repeat it again using the Z pair's average.
None of the fourteen is individually "correct" or "wrong" — comparing them to A0 and to each other is a robustness check: the closer they all agree, the less the measured anisotropy depends on any one orientation or any one axis's pairing.

AMS_Py & the Kappabridge

.pmagani isn't only written by STARpaleomag_Py's own 6-position Anisotropy tensor… dialog (TRM/ARM anisotropy, code2 A0/F0). AMS_Py — the companion application for standard anisotropy-of-magnetic-susceptibility work — reads a plain-text measurement report straight off an AGICO Kappabridge (a .asc file, the instrument's own software output) and writes the resulting tensor into the exact same .pmagani file, tagged code2 = N0 (susceptibility). The two applications share one column schema byte-for-byte, so a .pmagani file can freely carry A0/F0 rows from STARpaleomag_Py and N0 rows from AMS_Py side by side for the same specimen, and either application can read a row the other one wrote.

Straight through to MagIC. Once AMS data is sitting in .pmagani this way, AMS_Py's own Export to Magic command turns it directly into a MagIC specimens.txt file: each A0/F0/N0 row becomes one specimen record with a trace-normalized aniso_s tensor (s1:s2:s3:s4:s5:s6, summing the diagonal to 1 — the convention PmagPy itself uses) and the matching MagIC experiment type — N0 → AMS / LP-X, A0 → ATRM / LP-AN-TRM, F0 → AARM / LP-AN-ARM. Site and sample names for each specimen are looked up from the companion .prmag (opened once via Select site… in AMS_Py) rather than typed in twice — a specimen with no matching .prmag entry is still exported, just flagged with an empty site/sample so the gap is visible rather than guessed at. The 14 jackknife variants (A+/A-/A1…B6) are read-only diagnostics and are never turned into MagIC rows of their own.

f1 / f2 explained

What they measure. f1 and f2 do not repeat Coe's own f (the fraction of NRM removed within the chosen interval). They measure how much of the specimen's entire demagnetization history lies outside the interval, on each side — a check on whether the interval used for the intensity estimate is representative of the whole experiment, or leaves out a large share of the available decay.

Both are fractions of a vector difference sum (VDS) built across every measured step, from the true initial NRM through to the last point of the interval — not just the interval's two endpoints:

  • f1 — the share of that total VDS path still remaining once the interval's first point is reached. Close to 1 means the interval starts near the very beginning of the demagnetization sequence (little unused early decay).
  • f2 — the share remaining at the interval's last point. Close to 0 means the interval extends nearly to the end of the sequence (little unused late decay).

An interval with f1 near 1 and f2 near 0 uses essentially the specimen's whole usable decay — the most defensible choice, all else being equal. A low f1 or a high f2 flags that a sizeable part of the demagnetization was left out of the fit, worth a second look even when the interval's own statistics (f, g, q, MAD…) look fine on their own.
